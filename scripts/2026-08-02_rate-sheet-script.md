# The Rate Sheet Script for Sunday, August 2, 2026

Good morning team. Here is your Sunday, August 2, 2026 Rate Sheet.

The market move is this: we are heading into Monday with mortgage rates still pinned near their recent highs. Mortgage News Daily had its 30-year fixed index at 6.83% on Friday, July 31, after saying rates were back near long-term highs. In the bond market, the Fed's H.15 release showed the 10-year Treasury at 4.68% on Thursday, July 30, up from 4.61% on Tuesday, and the 30-year at 5.21%, up from 5.09%. That is the story to carry into the new week: long-term borrowing costs are still under pressure, and mortgage pricing has not found real relief yet.

Why it matters is that the market still does not believe inflation risk is fully behind us. The Fed held the funds rate at 3.50% to 3.75% on Wednesday, July 29, but it did so on a 9 to 3 vote, with three dissents for a hike. The statement said inflation remains elevated and called out energy-driven price pressure directly. Then Friday's inflation and labor-cost data gave the market no clean reason to relax. June PCE inflation cooled to 3.7% year over year and core PCE to 3.3%, which is better than May, but still too high to feel like victory. The Employment Cost Index also showed wage and salary growth up 0.9% for the quarter and 3.4% from a year ago. In plain terms: inflation is cooler, not cool.

What this means in plain English: the Fed paused, but the bond market is still acting like rates may need to stay higher for longer. Newer LOs should hear it this way: mortgage rates are not waiting for one Fed headline. They are reacting to inflation, wages, energy, and investor confidence all at once. Veterans can say it more directly: this is still a term-premium and inflation-expectations problem, so rate sheets are staying defensive even without an actual hike.

What to say to customers: "The market has not given us a clean rate break yet. The Fed didn't raise this week, but mortgage pricing still finished the week under pressure because investors are not fully comfortable with inflation. If the payment works and the home works, we should make a smart decision off today's market instead of waiting on a headline that may not help."

Lock-or-float guidance: for closings inside 15 days, lock. For closings inside 30 days, the bias is still lock unless the borrower has real flexibility and understands the risk. For 45 days and beyond, floating can still be reasonable, but only with active follow-up because this week has enough event risk to move bonds quickly. Sunday is not the time to promise a Monday improvement. The safer posture is disciplined, not hopeful.

Key events and times: Monday, August 3, the ISM Manufacturing PMI is due at 10:00 a.m. Eastern, and the Fed's Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey is due at 2:00 p.m. Eastern. Tuesday, August 4, the U.S. international trade report is due. Wednesday, August 5, the ISM Services PMI is due at 10:00 a.m. Eastern. Friday, August 7, the July jobs report lands at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, and Fed consumer credit data is due at 3:00 p.m. Eastern. Friday morning is the main risk event, but Monday can absolutely set the tone.

Market and housing context: affordability is still the drag, and demand is feeling it. Freddie Mac's weekly survey put the average 30-year fixed at 6.66% for the week ending July 30, up from 6.58% the prior week. Redfin said on July 30 that pending home sales fell to their lowest level since early April in the four weeks ending July 26, down 1.7% in just one week. Realtor.com said active inventory stayed above 1.1 million homes in the week ending July 25 and was up 2.7% year over year. That is the setup: buyers are cautious, but they also have more choices and more negotiating room than they had in the tighter markets of the last few years.

Competitive context for online quote objections: this market actually helps our argument if we use it correctly. When a borrower says, "I saw a lower rate online," the answer is: "You probably saw a teaser built around points, a narrow credit box, or a snapshot that moved before you finished reading it. In a volatile market, the real question is not the headline. It is what your payment, cash to close, and execution look like today." The sharper version for experienced LOs is simple: volatility widens the gap between advertised pricing and executable pricing. Use that.

Pipeline play, with the required refi angle: today is a database and follow-up day. Call stalled purchase shoppers first, especially the ones who paused over payment shock in the last 30 to 60 days. Then call past clients with FHA loans, recent builder buydowns, adjustable debt, or high-rate second liens. The refi angle is not "rates are about to fall." The refi angle is "let's get your numbers current now so when the window opens, you are first in line instead of starting from scratch." That keeps you credible and gives customers a reason to stay engaged even in a high-rate market.

One rookie mistake to avoid: do not tell borrowers that because inflation cooled a little, rates should come down soon. That is too loose and too simplistic. Inflation improved, but it is still above target, wage pressure is still firm, and the market just spent the week proving it needs more than one softer datapoint.

One advanced angle: separate payment pain from negotiation opportunity. In this market, higher rates are hurting urgency, but softer demand is improving leverage. That means a strong LO can shift the conversation from "Is the rate perfect?" to "Can we win better terms on the house, structure the payment intelligently, and preserve a refinance option later?" That is a better frame than arguing over an eighth.

Borrower talk tracks: "The Fed standing still did not automatically improve mortgage pricing." "You do not shop this market by headline rate alone; you shop it by total cost and payment strategy." "There are more homes and more negotiating room right now, so we can solve for the whole deal, not just the note rate." "If the right house is here now, we can make a smart move now and stay ready to improve the loan later."

One concrete borrower example: imagine a first-time buyer under contract at $410,000 who is nervous because they saw a flashy online ad on Saturday night. Your answer can be: "I get why that caught your eye, but this week the real market actually got a little worse, not better. Let's compare the ad to your real scenario, including points and cash to close. If this home still fits your life and your budget, locking protects the plan. If rates ease later, we can look at the next move then." That keeps the borrower calm and keeps you in the advisor seat.

One thing to watch: Monday's ISM Manufacturing report at 10:00 a.m. Eastern. If it shows firmer prices or stronger activity than expected, the market could carry last week's defensive tone straight into the new week. If it comes in softer, bonds may finally get a little room to recover. Either way, that release is the first real test of whether Friday's pressure extends or fades.

Coaching close for the team: your edge this week is interpretation. Anyone can repeat that rates are high. The better LO explains why they are high, what that means for a real borrower, and how to turn a frustrating market into a clear plan. Be steady, be specific, and keep every purchase conversation attached to a future refinance strategy without sounding like you're selling hope.
