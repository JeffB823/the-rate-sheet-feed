# The Rate Sheet Script

Saturday, August 22, 2026

Good morning team. Here is your Rate Sheet script for Saturday, August 22nd.

**Market move**

The market finished Friday, August 21st with mortgage pricing still under pressure, even though the move was not a full-blown selloff. Mortgage News Daily’s daily survey put the 30-year fixed at 6.77%, up one basis point from 6.76% the day before. At the same time, the 10-year Treasury closed around 4.69%, up from 4.65% on August 19th and back above where it was a week earlier. The simple read is that rates did not break higher in a dramatic way on Friday, but they also did not get meaningful relief.

**Why it matters**

That matters because this is the kind of market that wears borrowers out. We are not dealing with a clean rally that creates easy refinance conversations, and we are not dealing with a panic spike that forces obvious urgency. We are in the harder middle ground where small rate moves still hit affordability, monthly payment sensitivity stays high, and shoppers keep asking whether they should wait for something better.

**What this means in plain English**

In plain English, financing is still expensive enough to change behavior, but not stable enough to make borrowers feel comfortable. When rates stay elevated and bounce around inside a tight range, buyers hesitate, sellers keep negotiating, and customers start over-shopping because they think one more click will uncover a dramatically better deal. Usually it does not. Usually it just creates delay.

**What to say to customers**

Here is the language to use: “The market didn’t worsen enough to create panic, but it also didn’t improve enough to justify gambling with your payment. If the house works and the payment works, the smart move is to make a decision from certainty, not from hope.”

For a purchase lead, try this: “Rates are still moving with the bond market day to day, so the real question is not whether we can predict the perfect day. The real question is whether today’s payment fits your budget and keeps your plan intact.”

**Lock-or-float guidance**

For purchases closing inside 30 days, the bias is still toward locking. This market is proving that long-term yields can reprice higher quickly, and borrowers are not being paid much for the risk of waiting.

For closings in the 31-to-45-day window, I would call it a cautious lock bias unless the borrower has strong payment flexibility and understands the risk.

For floats beyond that, keep it disciplined. Floating right now should be a managed decision tied to timeline and tolerance, not a vibe. If a borrower would be frustrated by even a modest worsening in cost or payment, they are not really a float candidate.

**Key events and times**

The next real catalysts are next week, not today. On Tuesday, August 25th, we get Consumer Confidence and New Residential Sales at 10:00 a.m. Eastern. On Wednesday, August 26th, we get Durable Goods, the second estimate of Q2 GDP, and July Personal Income and Outlays with PCE at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Then on Friday, August 28th, we get final University of Michigan consumer sentiment at 10:00 a.m. Eastern, and Fed Chair Kevin Warsh is scheduled to speak at Jackson Hole at 10:00 a.m. Eastern.

That means Wednesday morning and next Friday morning are the two windows to respect. If you have borrowers who need certainty, do not let them drift casually into those events.

**Market and housing context**

The broader backdrop still says affordability is the issue, not demand disappearing forever. Freddie Mac’s weekly survey showed the 30-year fixed averaging 6.65% as of August 20th, down slightly from 6.67% a week earlier, so the bigger trend is not a fresh breakout but a stubbornly high plateau. Housing data also keeps showing a market that is active but payment-constrained. Existing-home sales in July slipped 1.7%, while median price still rose to $431,400 and supply sat at 4.6 months. New-home sales for June ran at a 628,000 annual pace, with a 9.3-month supply and a median price of $398,300. Redfin’s June national view still showed prices up 2.2% year over year.

Put that together and the story is straightforward: inventory has improved from the starvation levels of the last few years, but affordability is still doing the heavy lifting. Homes are available. Payments are the filter.

Borrower sentiment is also softening. The University of Michigan said consumer sentiment fell about 8% in August, with expected business conditions down sharply. That tells you your borrowers are not just comparing rates. They are comparing uncertainty.

**Competitive context for online quote objections**

When a borrower says, “I saw something lower online,” do not race to defend your rate sheet. Slow the conversation down. The better response is: “That may be a real quote, or it may be a headline rate built around points, a shorter lock, or an idealized scenario. Let’s compare the actual cash-to-close, payment, and risk side by side.”

That is the move. Online competition wins when we argue price in the abstract. We win when we translate structure, certainty, and execution into plain English. A teaser quote is not a mortgage strategy.

**Pipeline play with required refi angle**

The pipeline play today is not “blast a refi pitch.” It is “reopen payment-improvement conversations with borrowers who gave up when rates failed to fall fast enough.” Call past customers with high-rate second-half 2025 and early-2026 loans, especially anyone with FHA or VA profiles, monthly debt pressure, or upcoming cash-flow events.

Your angle is not “rates crashed.” They did not. Your angle is: “I’m not calling because the market is amazing. I’m calling because small improvements, term changes, debt consolidation, or removing mortgage insurance can still matter if your monthly budget is tight.” That is a more credible refinance conversation in this market.

**One rookie mistake to avoid**

Do not talk like one headline changes the whole market. Newer LOs get into trouble when they say things like, “Rates should come down next week,” just because a data release is coming. Say what is true: the market has event risk next week, and that can help or hurt. Credibility compounds faster than optimism.

**One advanced angle**

For experienced originators, this is a good market to separate nominal rate from borrower outcome. If the 10-year stays elevated because term premium and debt supply concerns remain sticky, then the sharper conversation is not “waiting for the Fed to cut.” It is “the Fed does not directly set mortgage rates, and long-end bond pressure can keep mortgage pricing stubborn even when short-rate expectations soften.” That explanation instantly upgrades the conversation with analytical borrowers.

**Borrower talk tracks**

Use this with a nervous first-time buyer: “I would love to tell you there’s a perfect week coming, but nobody can promise that honestly. What we can control is whether this payment is comfortable, whether the home fits, and whether you have a future plan if rates improve later.”

Use this with a payment shopper: “The cheapest quote on a screen is not automatically the lowest-cost loan in real life. Let’s compare total cost, time to close, and the odds the quote survives underwriting.”

Use this with a hesitant refi prospect: “This may not be the refi that makes headlines, but it could still be the one that improves your monthly life.”

**One concrete borrower example**

Say you have a borrower under contract at $425,000 who is putting 10% down and is already near the top of their comfort zone. In that case, a small worsening in market pricing matters more than the dream of squeezing out an eighth lower later. The practical advice is to lock the payment they can live with, then keep the future plan simple: if the market meaningfully improves after closing, we revisit the refinance conversation from a position of ownership instead of uncertainty.

**One thing to watch**

Watch whether next week’s PCE data and Warsh’s Jackson Hole remarks reinforce the idea that inflation and long-end yields stay sticky. If they do, the market may keep treating any rate improvement as temporary. If they come in softer or calmer than feared, we could finally get some breathing room. Either way, next week has more repricing potential than this weekend.

**Coaching close for the team**

Your edge right now is interpretation. Borrowers do not need another person reciting headlines at them. They need someone who can turn market noise into a clear decision. Be the loan officer who makes the next step feel simpler.
