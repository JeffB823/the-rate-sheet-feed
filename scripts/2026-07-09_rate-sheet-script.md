# The Rate Sheet Script for July 9, 2026

## Market move

Good morning. The market tone today is worse for rates than it looked earlier in the week. Mortgage News Daily said mortgage rates moved higher at a moderate pace over the last two days and were pushed closer to 10-month highs as geopolitical tension flared back up. In the Treasury market, the 10-year yield moved up again on Wednesday, July 8, to 4.56 from 4.55 on Tuesday, and the 5-year moved to 4.31 from 4.27. That is not a huge one-day jump, but it confirms that the bond market is still leaning defensive, and when bonds stay under pressure, mortgage pricing usually follows.

## Why it matters

This matters because mortgages do not really price off the Fed headline by itself. They price off the bond market, especially mortgage-backed securities and intermediate Treasuries. When investors get nervous about inflation risk, oil risk, deficit risk, or general headline risk, they demand better yields to own bonds. That means lenders usually have to offer worse pricing to borrowers. So even when there is no giant economic report on the screen, rates can still worsen just because the market wants more protection.

## What this means in plain English

In plain English, the market is reminding us that rates do not need a dramatic jobs report to move higher. Sometimes all it takes is a risk-off bond market and a reason for traders to think inflation could stay sticky a little longer. For borrowers, that means yesterday’s quote can get worse fast, even when the news feels unrelated to housing.

## What to say to customers

Here is the simple customer version: "Rates moved a little higher because the bond market got more cautious. Nothing is broken in your loan, but the market is charging more for certainty today than it was a couple of days ago. If payment matters, timing matters."

For a slightly sharper version: "Mortgage rates are being driven by bond traders, not by one lender getting greedy. When bond yields rise, every lender has to reprice into that market."

## Lock-or-float guidance

My guidance today is straightforward. If the borrower is inside 15 days, payment sensitive, or already close to their comfort ceiling, lock. If they are a purchase borrower with a thin DTI cushion, lock. If they are floating, they should have time, emotional tolerance, and a real reason to believe they can absorb some near-term volatility. Right now, the market has already shown a willingness to reprice worse on headlines, and the next major inflation report is close enough to be a real risk event.

## Key events and times

For today, the market is still digesting the Fed minutes released Wednesday, July 8 at 2:00 p.m. Eastern. The next scheduled macro event that really matters for mortgage pricing is the Consumer Price Index on Tuesday, July 14, 2026 at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Freddie Mac’s weekly survey is normally released on Thursdays at 12:00 p.m. Eastern, but remember that survey averages the prior week and often lags the feel of same-day rate sheets. For real-time rate conversations, the live bond market matters more.

## Market and housing context

Housing is still giving borrowers a mixed picture. MBA said mortgage applications fell 2.2% in the latest weekly survey released July 8, which tells you higher costs are still capping demand. Redfin’s latest weekly housing update said pending sales rose 0.4% week over week, but monthly payments also ticked up as home prices hit another record high. That is the market we are in: buyers are still active, but affordability is fragile. A small rate move still changes behavior.

## Competitive context for online quote objections

When a borrower says, "I saw a lower rate online," do not argue with the screenshot. Reframe it. "You probably did see a lower headline rate. The real question is whether that quote matches your credit profile, occupancy, down payment, lock period, and cost structure. A lot of online quotes are best-case marketing numbers. My job is to show you the real payment and the real cash-to-close, not just the prettiest rate on page one."

That keeps you out of a race to the bottom and back in advisor mode.

## Pipeline play with required refi angle

Do not assume the refi window is dead just because rates are not falling today. Shift the angle. This is still a strong day to sort your database into three buckets: cash-out borrowers carrying revolving debt, FHA and VA borrowers who may have future streamline potential, and recent shoppers who lost a home and may re-enter if inventory gives them another shot. The refi conversation right now is less about "drop your rate today" and more about "build a payment strategy and be first when the window opens."

A clean line for the call is: "I’m not calling because today is the perfect refi day. I’m calling because the next good refi usually goes to the borrower who already did the math before the window opens."

## One rookie mistake to avoid

Rookie mistake: quoting confidence when the market is unstable. Do not tell a borrower, "Rates should get better if you wait a few days," unless you are comfortable owning that advice if the market moves against them tomorrow. Explain the setup, explain the risk, and let the borrower make an informed choice.

## One advanced angle

Advanced angle: use the gap between weekly survey averages and live rate-sheet movement to sound smarter without sounding complicated. Freddie’s weekly average can look calmer than what retail loan officers feel in the moment, because the survey is backward-looking. That lets you say, "The weekly average may not look dramatic, but the tradeable market has already shifted, and lenders price off where bonds are trading now, not where the average sat earlier in the week."

## Borrower talk tracks

"If you love the house and the payment works, I would rather protect the payment than gamble for an eighth."

"The market does not send an invitation before it reprices. If this payment already fits, locking is how we keep it from getting away."

"You can always refinance a better market later. You cannot recover a house you lost because you hesitated on a workable payment."

## One concrete borrower example

Here is a real-world way to frame it. Say you have a first-time buyer who is already stretching to stay under budget and needs seller help to keep cash-to-close manageable. For that borrower, even a modest market worsening can push the payment or cash needed in the wrong direction. The right advice is not, "Let’s hope for a miracle." The right advice is, "You have a home, a payment that still works, and a market that has been moving against you this week. Let’s protect the win."

## One thing to watch

Watch whether bond traders keep treating inflation risk as sticky even without fresh hard data. If that continues, mortgage pricing can stay defensive into next week and the CPI setup can become a bigger lock conversation every day we get closer to Tuesday morning.

Today is a teach-and-close day. Teach the borrower why the market moved, close the gap between headline shopping and real loan structure, and ask directly for the decision while the payment still works.
