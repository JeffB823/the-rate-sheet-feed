# The Rate Sheet Script - August 7, 2026

Good morning. Here is your Rate Sheet for Friday, August 7.

**Market move**

The market move this morning is defensive, not friendly. Bonds weakened again on Thursday, the 10-year Treasury moved up into the mid-4.60s, and Mortgage News Daily's daily survey put the top tier 30-year fixed at 6.77% on August 6, up a touch from 6.75% the day before. Freddie Mac's weekly survey also moved higher, with the average 30-year fixed at 6.69% as of Thursday, August 6. So the headline is simple: rates are still leaning higher, and the market is walking into payrolls with very little cushion.

**Why it matters**

That matters because when rates are already elevated and bonds are selling off before a major data release, borrowers feel every extra bit of uncertainty. This is the kind of setup where one strong labor headline can push lenders worse quickly, while a softer report could finally give the market room to breathe. In other words, today is less about where rates were yesterday and more about how exposed they are at 8:30 a.m. Eastern.

**What this means in plain English**

Plain English: mortgage rates are not in free fall, but they are still expensive, and the market is nervous. Think of it like a spring under pressure. If today's jobs report comes in hotter than expected, rates can snap higher fast. If the report shows a cooler labor market, borrowers may finally get a little relief. Until then, this is a wait-and-react morning.

**What to say to customers**

Say this: "We're heading into one of those market mornings where the next economic report can change pricing fast. Rates are still on the high side, so my job today is to help you make a decision based on your timeline, not on guesswork."

**Lock-or-float guidance**

If the borrower is closing inside 15 days, I would lean lock. There is not enough reward in gambling on a perfect jobs number when the market already has inflation and Fed worries in the background. If they are 15 to 30 days out and have room to absorb volatility, floating into the report can be reasonable, but only if you have already told them the risk is real and immediate. For shoppers, do not sell predictions. Sell readiness.

**Key events and times**

The big event is the July jobs report from BLS at 8:30 a.m. Eastern on Friday, August 7. Traders will care about payroll growth, the unemployment rate, wage pressure, and revisions. If the data comes in strong, the market will likely read that as one more reason the Fed can stay tough. If it comes in softer, bonds have a chance to recover. After that, keep an eye on the University of Michigan preliminary August sentiment release next Friday, August 14 at 10:00 a.m. Eastern for the next read on consumer psychology and inflation expectations.

**Market and housing context**

The backdrop is still rate-unfriendly. Thursday's weekly jobless claims came in at 199,000 for the week ending August 1, which is low enough to say layoffs are still contained. That does not help bonds much because it keeps the labor market from looking weak enough to force a friendlier Fed path. On housing, Redfin said pending home sales sank 3.7% week over week in data published August 6, the biggest weekly drop since 2022, as buyers hit pause under higher rates. Redfin also said the income needed to afford the typical home is still hovering near a record high, around $110,000. So the story is not just high rates. It is high rates meeting payment fatigue.

**Competitive context for online quote objections**

When a borrower says, "I found a lower quote online," keep it calm. Say, "You may have found a lower starting number, but the real test is whether it holds after points, fees, escrows, insurance, credit, and timeline. In this market, the cheapest headline quote is not always the cheapest loan." The point is to move the conversation away from teaser pricing and back to execution.

**Pipeline play with required refi angle**

Today's pipeline play is a refinance reality check. Pull FHA, VA, and conventional loans from the last 18 months where the borrower landed with a payment they tolerated but never liked. In this market, the refi pitch is not "rates are way down." That is not true. The angle is: can we improve monthly cash flow, consolidate higher-rate debt, remove mortgage insurance, or shorten term without blowing up the budget? Refi volume is still selective, but the borrowers worth calling are the ones who need a better monthly picture, not a magic headline rate.

**One rookie mistake to avoid**

Rookie mistake: telling borrowers to wait because "rates might come down after jobs." Maybe they will. Maybe they will not. The mistake is acting like hope is a strategy. Your job is to explain the tradeoff clearly, not to sound certain when the market is anything but certain.

**One advanced angle**

Advanced angle: tie weak housing demand to stronger negotiation leverage. If pending sales are slipping while listings are still available, the financing conversation should include seller credits, buydowns, and repair asks, not just rate watching. The sharp LO today is not only quoting rate. They are showing how market softness can be turned into structure.

**Borrower talk tracks**

"You do not need perfect timing. You need a plan that still works if the market stays choppy."

"If rates improve after the report, great, we can react. If they do not, I want you protected instead of surprised."

"The internet is good at showing numbers. My value is showing you which number actually survives the full loan process."

**One concrete borrower example**

Here is a clean example. Say a buyer is looking at a $425,000 loan and is nervous because the monthly payment feels stretched. Instead of debating every eighth of a point, show them two paths: one with today's market rate and one with a seller-paid temporary buydown or credit. That changes the conversation from "Should I wait for the market to save me?" to "How do I structure this deal so it works now?"

**One thing to watch**

Watch the reaction, not just the headline, after 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Sometimes payrolls, unemployment, or wages send mixed signals, and the bond market cares most about the part that changes Fed expectations. The first read is important, but the real clue is whether lenders keep repricing worse or the market settles down after the first move.

**Coaching close for the team**

Be the calm person in the room today. A nervous market creates a real opening for the loan officer who can explain risk without sounding dramatic, explain options without overpromising, and turn uncertainty into a plan the borrower can actually act on.
