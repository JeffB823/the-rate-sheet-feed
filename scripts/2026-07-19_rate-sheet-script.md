# The Rate Sheet Script - July 19, 2026

Market move. Mortgage pricing got a little better into the Friday close, but let’s not oversell it. Bonds finished the week slightly better than they started, and Mortgage News Daily showed the average 30-year fixed moving down to 6.63% on Friday, July 17, after touching higher levels earlier in the week. Even with that late-week recovery, Freddie Mac's weekly survey still printed 6.55% on July 16, which is the highest weekly average in nearly a year. The 10-year Treasury closed Friday, July 17, at 4.55%, down a couple of basis points from Thursday, but still high enough to keep rate sheets under pressure.

Why it matters. This market is telling us that one or two softer inflation prints helped, but they did not fully reset the bigger rate story. Mortgage money improved at the margin because bonds liked the cooler inflation data earlier in the week. But lenders are still pricing in inflation risk, energy risk, and the idea that the Fed is not ready to sound relaxed yet. So yes, pricing got friendlier by the end of the week. No, this is not a clean break into a lower-rate cycle.

What this means in plain English. If a borrower asks, "Did rates get better?" the honest answer is yes, a little. If they ask, "Are we back to the good old days?" the answer is no. We got some relief off the worst levels of the week, but borrowing costs are still elevated enough to change affordability, debt-to-income, and payment shock conversations in real time.

What to say to customers. Try this: "We did get a little market relief going into the weekend, but rates are still being pulled around by inflation expectations and the bond market. So the win right now is not chasing some perfect headline rate. The win is making sure we structure the loan smartly and move when the market gives us an opening."

Lock-or-float guidance. For borrowers closing inside 15 days, locking still makes the most sense. This market can improve a little and then give it right back on one oil headline or one hot data point. For 15 to 30 day closings, I would stay nimble and borrower-specific. If the payment works and the borrower is margin-tight on DTI or cash to close, lock the win. For longer timelines, floating can still be reasonable, but only if the borrower understands that this is a choppy market, not a one-way rally.

Key events and times. Monday, July 20, we get Leading Economic Indicators at 10:00 a.m. Eastern. Thursday, July 23, jobless claims hit at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Friday, July 24, we get S&P Global flash PMIs at 9:45 a.m. Eastern and new home sales at 10:00 a.m. Eastern. The next Fed meeting is July 28 to July 29, and the market still leans toward no change, which means traders will stay hypersensitive to anything that shifts inflation or growth expectations before then.

Market and housing context. Friday’s housing data was mixed in a way that actually helps explain borrower confusion. June housing starts jumped 19% month over month, but single-family starts were basically flat and single-family permits fell 2.4%. Builder sentiment also slipped again in July, down to 34, and more builders are cutting prices and leaning on incentives. Translation: builders are still building where they can, but the resale and new-home markets are both dealing with the same affordability wall. Higher rates are not freezing everything. They are just making every buyer more payment-sensitive.

Competitive context for online quote objections. When borrowers wave around an online quote, do not fight the headline rate with another headline rate. Move the conversation to execution. Say: "A screenshot is not a strategy. The real question is what happens to that quote after points, LLPAs, escrow setup, condo review, appraisal gap, and closing timeline. Low-advertised pricing is common. Clean closings at the quoted payment are less common." In this market, certainty is part of the value proposition.

Pipeline play with the required refi angle. Purchase demand is still soft, but refi opportunity has not disappeared. MBA's latest weekly application data showed overall volume down while refinance activity still rose modestly. That is the signal. Your refi play is not broad-market blasting. It is precision. Call the borrowers who closed with higher FHA or VA rates, the conventional borrowers carrying large monthly MI, and the cash-out candidates who used HELOCs or credit cards to bridge a move. The pitch is not "rates crashed." The pitch is "your current structure may be beatable even in a market that still feels high."

One rookie mistake to avoid. Do not tell borrowers to wait for the Fed as if the Fed meeting itself is a coupon special. Newer LOs keep saying, "Let’s see what the Fed does," when the real driver for mortgage pricing is the bond market’s view of inflation, growth, and risk. If you train borrowers to stare at the Fed headline, you set yourself up to look wrong when the Fed holds steady and mortgage pricing still moves.

One advanced angle. The sharper conversation this week is that rates improved even while the weekly Freddie number moved higher. That gives experienced LOs a useful distinction: the survey average is backward-looking, while live lock pricing reacts faster. In other words, the story you tell your borrower today should be based on current market tone, not a stale weekly average that captured the uglier part of the week.

Borrower talk tracks. "You do not need the lowest rate in America. You need the best executable option for your timeline and budget." "If the payment is workable today, we can protect the house first and improve the financing later if the market gives us that chance." "In this environment, the cost of missing the right house can be bigger than the cost of being a little early on the rate."

One concrete borrower example. Think about a first-time buyer who is approved around the edge on DTI and is shopping against an online lender. If Friday's market improvement saves even a fraction on payment, that can be the difference between keeping the borrower in a conventional approval versus falling back to a more expensive structure. The right move is not to promise a rally. The right move is to show them two clean side-by-side options today, lock if the payment clears the hurdle, and keep the emotional focus on certainty instead of regret.

One thing to watch. Watch oil and inflation expectations more than stock headlines this week. University of Michigan sentiment improved in July, but year-ahead inflation expectations were still elevated at 4.2%, and the survey period mostly missed the later jump in Iran-related tensions and gas prices. That means sentiment may look better on paper than borrowers actually feel if energy prices keep climbing. If gas spikes again, rate optimism can fade fast.

Coaching close for the team. Your job this week is not to predict the next tenth in rate. Your job is to translate volatility into confidence. Be the LO who can explain the market simply, lock decisively when the deal works, and reopen the refi conversation before your competitors remember those borrowers exist.
