# The Rate Sheet Script for Sunday, August 16, 2026

Good morning team. Here is the market setup for Sunday, August 16, 2026, heading into Monday.

The market move is basically this: mortgage rates did not get a clean victory lap from softer inflation and weaker retail sales. Freddie Mac's weekly survey for August 13 showed the average 30-year fixed at 6.67%, down a hair from 6.69%, but Mortgage News Daily said rates finished Friday, August 14, just a touch higher on the day and MBS were moderately weaker into the close. At the Treasury level, the 10-year yield closed Thursday, August 13 at 4.63% after being 4.72% on Monday, August 10, while the 30-year stayed elevated above 5.20%. That tells you the market liked the softer near-term inflation story, but it still does not fully trust the longer-run inflation and supply picture.

Why that matters is simple. Mortgage pricing does not move on one headline. It moves on the full bond-market argument. July CPI rose just 0.1% month over month and 3.4% year over year, with core CPI up 2.5% year over year. July PPI was unchanged month over month. July retail sales then fell 0.6%. In a cleaner market, that combination would usually give mortgage borrowers more obvious relief. Instead, the long end of the bond market stayed stubborn. So the lesson for clients is that cooler inflation helps, but it has not yet been enough to produce a lasting rate break.

What this means in plain English: the market is acting like a person who heard some good news but is still not ready to relax. Inflation data improved. Consumer spending softened. But investors still want extra compensation to own longer-term bonds, and mortgage rates live in that longer-term world. That is why your borrower can hear "inflation cooled" on the news and still not see a dramatic improvement in today's quote.

What to say to customers: "We did get some better inflation news this week, and that helped keep rates from pushing even higher. But mortgage rates follow the bond market, not just one report, so we're still in a range rather than a true downward trend. My job is to help you move when the market gives us an opening, not chase headlines one day late."

Lock or float guidance: for borrowers closing in the next 15 to 30 days, this still looks like a lock-biased market. The improvement story is real, but it is not yet durable. For 45-day borrowers with strong flexibility, floating can still make sense if Monday and Tuesday data reinforce the softer-growth theme, but that should be an active float, not a passive hope trade. If a client would be upset by losing an eighth in rate or meaningful lender credit, they are not a real float candidate.

Key events and times: Monday, August 17 brings the NAHB Housing Market Index at 10:00 a.m. Eastern. Tuesday, August 18 brings housing starts and building permits at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, and pending home sales at 10:00 a.m. Eastern. Wednesday, August 19 brings the FOMC minutes from the July 28-29 meeting at 2:00 p.m. Eastern. Those minutes matter because the market is still trying to decide whether the next Fed move is another hike or an extended hold. CME FedWatch as of Saturday, August 15 showed the market leaning away from a September hike versus earlier in the week, but not ruling it out.

Market and housing context: housing is still functioning under pressure, not collapsing. Existing-home sales for July fell 1.7% to a 4.06 million annual pace, while the median price rose to $434,100. That is the exact pain point your buyers feel: payment pressure is high, supply is still not normal, and prices are not falling enough to offset the rate shock. At the same time, the University of Michigan's preliminary August consumer sentiment reading dropped to 51.0 from 55.2, with expectations down to 50.6. Borrowers are telling you directly that confidence is thin. That does not kill demand, but it does increase hesitation, second-guessing, and quote shopping.

Competitive context for online quote objections: when a borrower says, "I saw a better rate online," do not fight over the teaser. Reframe the conversation around certainty. "Online quotes are often best-case snapshots tied to ideal credit, ideal equity, and timing that may not match your file. What matters is the real execution on your scenario, the speed to close, and whether the structure still makes sense if the market moves before you lock." In this kind of market, the professional edge is not just the note rate. It is preventing a borrower from making a false comparison between a headline ad and an actual approval path.

Pipeline play, and this one must include the refi angle: do not wait for a full refi boom to start refi conversations. Build a rate-watch pipeline now from borrowers who closed in the past 12 to 24 months with higher debt loads, recent home-improvement balances, second-lien clutter, or mortgage insurance that may be removable with updated value. The pitch is not "rates are way down." That would be weak and probably false for many files. The pitch is "we may be able to improve monthly cash flow, simplify debt, or shorten your timeline once the market gives us enough room." Refi demand returns in layers. Cash-flow relief and debt cleanup usually show up before the broad headline-wave refinance market does.

One rookie mistake to avoid: do not tell borrowers that the Fed controls mortgage rates directly. Newer loan officers still do this, and it creates confusion the second mortgage pricing fails to move with a Fed headline. A better line is: "The Fed influences the environment, but mortgage rates track the bond market more directly, especially longer-term yields and mortgage-backed securities."

One advanced angle: the curve is doing some of the work for you in the conversation. Shorter-term rate expectations eased after CPI, PPI, and retail sales, but longer-term yields stayed relatively firm. That is a clean way to explain why headlines felt friendlier than pricing. For experienced LOs, the sharper client language is: "The market is softening on growth, but not yet fully repricing long-term inflation risk." That sentence sounds smart because it is smart, and it matches the tape.

Borrower talk tracks:

"If you're buying now, the win is not waiting for a perfect rate that may never show up on your timeline. The win is structuring the payment correctly and staying ready to improve it later if the market opens up."

"If you need certainty, we should respect that and lock. If you have flexibility, we can float tactically, but only with a clear trigger and a clear stop."

"The market gave us enough this week to stay constructive, not enough to get complacent."

"A cheaper online quote is only better if it is real, available for your file, and still there when you need to lock."

One concrete borrower example: imagine a first-time buyer under contract at $410,000 with 10% down who is payment-sensitive and nervous after seeing mixed rate headlines. The wrong move is saying, "Let's float because inflation came in better." The better move is: "This week helped stabilize the market, but we are still in a choppy range. Since your closing is inside 21 days and your payment comfort matters more than gambling for marginal improvement, locking here protects the home and keeps us ready to renegotiate if the market meaningfully improves before docs."

One thing to watch: if next week's housing data softens and the FOMC minutes sound less eager to tighten again, the market could test a friendlier tone for mortgages. But if the minutes reinforce inflation vigilance or long bonds keep backing up on supply and fiscal concerns, mortgage rates can stay stubborn even with softer consumer data. Watch the 10-year, but especially watch whether MBS actually outperform instead of merely surviving.

Coaching close for the team: your edge this week is interpretation. Do not just repeat that inflation cooled. Explain why that helped, why it did not fully translate, and what the borrower should do because of it. The LO who can turn mixed macro data into one calm, usable recommendation will win business from the LO who only reads headlines.
