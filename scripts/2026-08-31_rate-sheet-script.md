# The Rate Sheet Script for Monday, August 31, 2026

Good morning. Here is your rate sheet for Monday, August 31, 2026.

Market move. Mortgage pricing is opening the week on the defensive after Friday, August 28, when Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivered a hawkish Jackson Hole speech and pushed the average top-tier 30-year fixed rate up to 6.81% at Mortgage News Daily. Early Monday, August 31, the 10-year Treasury is near 4.72%, which tells us the bond market is not in free fall this morning, but it is still carrying Friday's damage.

Why it matters. Loan pricing does not need a fresh selloff to stay worse. If bonds get hit hard on Friday and do not recover, lenders often start the next session with that weaker tone still embedded in rate sheets. That is the setup today. We are not talking about a panic move. We are talking about a market that just got a reminder that the Fed is still more worried about inflation than it is eager to rescue long-term rates.

What this means in plain English. For borrowers, this is the kind of day where rate shoppers may say, "I thought rates were supposed to be coming down." Your answer is simple: mortgage rates do not move on headlines about hope. They move when bond investors feel safer owning mortgage bonds. Right now, the Fed tone, sticky inflation backdrop, and higher oil prices are not giving that safety trade much help.

What to say to customers. "Rates are not blowing out this morning, but the market did get worse late last week and lenders are still carrying that repricing. If you are close to contract deadlines or payment comfort limits, this is a market to respect, not a market to gamble on."

Lock-or-float guidance. If the borrower closes in the next 15 to 30 days, lean lock unless they have meaningful cushion and strong stomach for volatility. If they are 45 days out or longer, float conversations can stay alive, but only with clear guardrails: tomorrow's data and Friday's jobs report can either help rates retrace some of Friday's move or push this market into another leg worse. Float is a strategy right now, not a default.

Key events and times. Today, Monday, August 31, is relatively light for major scheduled mortgage-moving data. The bigger volatility windows start tomorrow, Tuesday, September 1, at 10:00 a.m. ET with ISM Manufacturing and Construction Spending, and then Friday, September 4, at 8:30 a.m. ET with the August Employment Situation report. Those are the next clean catalysts for rate direction.

Market and housing context. Freddie Mac's weekly survey, released Thursday, August 27, showed the average 30-year fixed at 6.66%, basically steady week over week, but that survey does not fully capture Friday's Jackson Hole repricing. Housing itself still looks like a slower-demand, better-supply story. Redfin's August 27 update showed new listings rising to a four-month high while pending sales slipped to a six-month low. That matters because it gives buyers more room to negotiate even while financing costs stay uncomfortable.

Competitive context for online quote objections. When a borrower says, "But the online lender showed me something lower," do not fight the screenshot. Reframe the comparison. "A teaser quote is not the same thing as a locked loan with real timing, real escrows, real fees, and someone managing the file when the market turns at noon. In a volatile market, execution beats advertising." The sharper version for experienced LOs is this: low-friction quote engines win the first click, but trusted advisors win the fallout when assumptions break.

Pipeline play, with the refi angle. Today is a payment-review day. Call every active shopper who has gone quiet and every closed client from the last 18 months who took a rate above their comfort zone. The refi angle is not, "Rates are low again," because they are not. The angle is, "Let's map your break-even now so we can move fast when the window opens." Build the watchlist before the rally, not after it.

One rookie mistake to avoid. Do not tell borrowers, "The Fed doesn't set mortgage rates," and leave it there. That sounds technically smart and practically useless. Better version: "The Fed controls short-term policy, but what it says changes the whole bond market, and that bond market is what moves mortgage pricing."

One advanced angle. Watch the gap between a stable 10-year Treasury and sticky mortgage pricing. If Treasuries calm down but lender pricing does not improve much, that usually means mortgage spreads and lender caution are doing the damage. That is a strong signal to manage borrower expectations tightly because the Treasury headline alone may overpromise improvement.

Borrower talk tracks. "You do not need the perfect rate to make a good housing decision. You need a payment and plan that still work if the market stays messy for a bit." And: "If the house is right and the budget is right, we can solve for the rate later. If the payment is already too tight, waiting for a miracle eighth lower is not a real strategy."

One concrete borrower example. If a buyer is under contract at $425,000 and Friday's market shift moved the payment enough to create hesitation, do not jump straight into rate predictions. First ask whether seller credits, a small price adjustment, or a structure change can recover the monthly payment more reliably than hoping the bond market bails them out this week. That keeps the conversation in the borrower's control.

One thing to watch. Consumer sentiment is still weak. The University of Michigan's final August reading fell to 51.7, down from 55.2 in July, and inflation worries are leading the conversation more than unemployment. That tells you borrowers are likely to feel fragile even when their income qualifies. Emotional affordability is a real issue right now.

Coaching close for the team. Talk slower than the market today. Borrowers do not need a macro lecture. They need someone who can translate a hawkish Fed, a shaky bond market, and a still-negotiable housing market into one calm next step.
