# The Rate Sheet Script for Saturday, August 29, 2026

## Market move

Here’s the setup for today. Mortgage pricing heads into the weekend worse than it looked early Friday, August 28, because the bond market sold off hard after Fed Chair Kevin Warsh struck a firmer inflation tone at Jackson Hole. The 10-year Treasury closed around 4.73%, up from roughly 4.67% on Thursday, and mortgage-backed securities were hit as well. In plain terms, the bond market heard, "the Fed is still more worried about inflation than it is eager to rescue rate shoppers," and that pushed borrowing costs higher by the afternoon.

## Why it matters

That matters because mortgage rates do not wait for the Fed to actually move. They react to what traders think is coming next. When the market believes the Fed could stay tighter for longer, or even lean hawkish into the September 15-16 meeting, lenders usually protect themselves fast. So even though Freddie Mac’s weekly survey still showed the 30-year fixed basically steady at 6.66% on August 27, Friday’s late bond weakness tells you same-day or next-business-day rate sheets can still worsen underneath that headline.

## What this means in plain English

Plain English: the market just reminded borrowers that rates can move higher quickly when inflation fears wake back up. This is not a panic moment, but it is a reminder that the easy-money story is not back. If a borrower was hoping rates would simply drift lower because everyone wants them lower, Friday was a reality check.

## What to say to customers

What I’d say to customers is this: "Rates improved earlier this year, but they’re still being pushed around by inflation and the bond market. Friday was one of those days where the market got more defensive, so the best move is to make a decision based on your timeline, not on hope that Monday magically gets better."

## Lock-or-float guidance

My guidance is simple. If the borrower is inside 30 days, lean lock. If they are 31 to 45 days out, lock unless they have strong pricing cushion and can emotionally handle volatility. If they are farther out and well-qualified, floating can still be reasonable, but only if the conversation is disciplined: this is a managed float, not a wish. Friday’s selloff means the burden of proof shifts back to the float side.

## Key events and times

Here’s what matters next week. Tuesday, September 1 at 10:00 a.m. Eastern we get JOLTS job openings, ISM manufacturing, and Census construction spending. Wednesday, September 2 at 2:00 p.m. Eastern brings the Fed’s Beige Book. Friday, September 4 at 8:30 a.m. Eastern is the August jobs report, and that is the biggest near-term market mover on the calendar. Then the next Fed meeting is September 15-16. For pipeline conversations, the message is: the market now has several chances to keep rates jumpy before the Fed even meets.

## Market and housing context

Housing still looks soft on activity, not broken on demand. NAR said July pending home sales fell 2.3% from June and 2.2% from a year earlier, dropping to the lowest level since January 2026. MBA’s latest weekly application data also showed demand still stalled as rates moved up, with total apps down, purchase activity slightly lower, and refinance activity dampened by higher borrowing costs. At the same time, the buyer is still there underneath the surface. The issue is not that Americans stopped wanting homes. The issue is that affordability keeps interrupting action.

Consumer mood backs that up. The University of Michigan’s final August sentiment reading fell to 51.7 from 55.2 in July, with expectations also lower. That tells you borrowers are still price-sensitive, payment-sensitive, and headline-sensitive. They are not just shopping a house. They are shopping for emotional safety.

## Competitive context for online quote objections

When a borrower says, "I saw a lower rate online," don’t fight the quote. Translate it. Say: "You probably did see a lower note rate. The real question is what it costs to get it, whether it assumes perfect credit, a big down payment, discount points, or a closing timeline that doesn’t match your deal." In this market, the winning move is not to sound defensive. The winning move is to separate headline rate from executed payment, cash to close, and certainty to close.

## Pipeline play with the required refi angle

Your pipeline play today is not a broad "refi boom is back" message, because it isn’t. The smarter refi angle is targeted payment strategy. Call the past client with consumer debt, the homeowner who needs cash for a controlled purpose, the FHA borrower who may still benefit from a clean payment reset, and the borrower who can drop MI or shorten term without breaking budget. The pitch is not "rates are amazing." The pitch is "let’s see if your total monthly picture can still improve even in a choppy rate market."

## One rookie mistake to avoid

Rookie mistake: telling borrowers, "The Fed didn’t raise rates, so mortgage rates should come down." That is not how this works, and Friday was the proof. Mortgage rates move on expectations, inflation risk, Treasury yields, and MBS pricing. If you explain that clearly, you sound credible. If you oversimplify it, you sound surprised by your own rate sheet.

## One advanced angle

Advanced angle: use Friday’s move to create urgency without sounding like a rate chaser. Tell serious borrowers, "The market repriced on tone, not just data. That means conviction is thin and lenders are quick to defend pricing. In markets like this, execution matters more than prediction." That line works because it teaches the borrower something while also positioning you as the adult in the room.

## Borrower talk tracks

"I’m not trying to call the exact bottom in rates for you. I’m trying to help you make a good mortgage decision inside your real timeline."

"If the payment works and the home fits, we do not need a perfect market to make a smart move."

"An online quote can be a useful starting point, but the right comparison is total cost, monthly payment, and whether that quote still holds up once your actual file is underwritten."

## One concrete borrower example

Here’s a clean example. Say you have a move-up buyer under contract closing in three weeks. They saw a softer headline earlier this month and want to float because they think rates will come back. Friday’s market says that is a fragile bet. The better advice is: lock the loan, protect the payment, and keep shopping the home, not the fantasy of an eighth better rate showing up in time. If the market improves meaningfully and your process allows a better execution later, great. But today the bigger risk is losing control of the payment late in the deal.

## One thing to watch

Watch whether next week’s labor data keeps the market in this hawkish lane. If job openings and payrolls stay firm, traders may keep pushing yields higher and lenders will stay defensive. If labor data softens enough to cool Fed fears, some of Friday’s damage could retrace. So the real question is not "Will rates get better?" The real question is "Will the labor and inflation story give bonds room to breathe?"

## Coaching close for the team

Today’s coaching close is this: stop selling optimism and start selling clarity. In a market like this, the loan officer who calmly explains payment, timing, and tradeoffs will beat the loan officer who keeps promising a better tomorrow.
