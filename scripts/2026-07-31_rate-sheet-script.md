# The Rate Sheet Script for Friday, July 31, 2026

Good morning team. As of early Friday, July 31, 2026, the market move is this: mortgage rates are still sitting near their recent highs, but yesterday gave us a small break from the worst-case scenario. Mortgage News Daily had the average 30-year fixed at 6.77% on Thursday, down a hair from 6.78% on Wednesday. Freddie Mac's weekly survey, released Thursday, moved up to 6.66% from 6.58%. The bigger backdrop is still heavy. The 10-year Treasury closed at 4.67% on Wednesday, and the 30-year Treasury hit 5.20%. That tells you long-term borrowing costs are still elevated even when the daily mortgage quote barely moves.

Why it matters is simple. The Fed held the funds rate at 3.50% to 3.75% on Wednesday, July 29, 2026, but three policymakers wanted a hike, and the statement said inflation is still elevated, with energy called out directly. Then Thursday's inflation data helped a little without fully rescuing the market. June PCE cooled to 3.7% year over year from 4.1% in May, but that is still far from comfortable. So the message to the mortgage market was not "all clear." It was "maybe less bad, but still not easy."

What this means in plain English: rates did not blow out again yesterday, which is helpful, but they also did not meaningfully improve. Newer LOs should hear it this way: the market stopped the bleeding for a day, but the patient is not healthy yet. Veterans should hear the sharper version: we are in a high-rate market that is looking for permission to stay high unless wage and inflation data clearly roll over.

What to say to customers: "Yesterday was encouraging because rates did not get worse after the Fed scare, but we are still dealing with an expensive borrowing market. Inflation cooled some, but not enough to declare victory. If you're buying soon, the right question is whether the payment works now, not whether one softer report magically fixes mortgage pricing."

Lock-or-float guidance: for closings inside 15 days, lock. For closings inside 30 days, the bias is still lock unless the borrower is unusually flexible and understands the risk around this morning's data. At 8:30 a.m. Eastern today we get the Employment Cost Index, and at 10:00 a.m. Eastern we get final July University of Michigan consumer sentiment. Wage data matters because if compensation runs hot, the bond market can quickly decide inflation is not cooling fast enough. For borrowers 45 days or more out, you can justify a cautious float only if you have clear reprice discipline and a same-day communication plan.

Key events and times: today, Friday, July 31, 2026, the Employment Cost Index hits at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Final July University of Michigan sentiment is due at 10:00 a.m. Eastern. Next Friday, August 7, 2026, the July employment report lands at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. Those are the next checkpoints because the market has moved past the headline of "Fed held" and is back to asking whether inflation pressure, especially wage pressure, is still sticky.

Market and housing context: the consumer is softer, and housing demand is feeling it. The Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index slipped to 90.8 in July from 92.2 in June. MBA said mortgage applications fell 6.4% in the latest weekly survey. Redfin said homebuying demand is slowing as daily average mortgage rates pushed up to 6.85% late last week, the highest level in over a year. At the same time, Freddie Mac said more available inventory is still helping support buyer activity. That is the real setup: demand is more cautious, but serious buyers still have more choices than they had before.

Competitive context for online quote objections: use the spread between headline averages to teach, not defend. Freddie Mac's 6.66% weekly average and Mortgage News Daily's 6.77% daily reading are both real, but they are not the same thing. One is a weekly survey window and one is a more current daily market read. So when a borrower says, "I saw 6.25 online," the answer is: "Maybe you saw a teaser, a shorter lock, points baked in, or a borrower profile that is not yours. My job is to show you the rate, the cost, the payment, and the strategy together."

Pipeline play, with the required refi angle: today is a mortgage-planning call day, not a rate-drop fantasy day. Call past clients who bought in the last 12 to 24 months with higher debt, temporary buydowns, or FHA-to-conventional potential. Say: "I'm not calling because today is the refinance day. I'm calling because I want your numbers updated so if the market opens a window later, we move fast instead of starting from scratch." That keeps the refi bench alive while staying credible.

One rookie mistake to avoid: do not tell borrowers that cooler PCE means rates are coming down now. One better inflation print can calm the market, but it does not erase a Fed statement, three dissents, and high Treasury yields. If you overpromise relief, you will lose trust the moment pricing stalls or reverses.

One advanced angle: use today's cautious demand backdrop as a positioning tool for committed buyers. When demand softens but inventory improves, the conversation shifts from "Can we win at any price?" to "How do we structure the cleanest total payment?" Veterans can win by talking through seller credits, buydowns, and monthly payment durability instead of competing on eighths in rate.

Borrower talk tracks: "The market gave us a pause, not a pivot." "If the home works and the payment works, we can protect the opportunity now and stay ready to improve the financing later." "Online quotes are snapshots; your mortgage decision is a full strategy." "The goal is not to guess the perfect day. The goal is to make a strong decision with the market we actually have."

One concrete borrower example: picture a first-time buyer at $410,000 using 5% down, nervous because a big website flashed a lower rate than the one in your preapproval update. The right coaching is: show the difference between a headline ad and an actual quote, compare total cash and monthly payment under a straight note rate versus a seller-paid buydown, and explain that yesterday's steadier bond market is helpful but not enough to justify gambling with the contract timeline. That teaches the borrower and keeps the deal grounded.

One thing to watch: if the Employment Cost Index comes in hot this morning, the market may decide yesterday's calm was temporary and push long-term yields higher again. If wage data is cooler and sentiment stays weak, bonds may finally get a little more room to recover. Either way, the first read after 8:30 a.m. Eastern matters more than broad Fed commentary today.

Coaching close for the team: be precise today. Tell borrowers the market stabilized, not healed. Use plain English around inflation and wages, answer online quote objections with structure instead of emotion, and make every purchase call do double duty by planting a smart future refi conversation.
