# The Rate Sheet Script for Tuesday, August 25, 2026

As of 6:31 a.m. Eastern on Tuesday, August 25, 2026, the market move is basically this: mortgage rates are still living near the upper end of their recent range, but the early tone this morning is a little calmer than the fear we saw late last week. Mortgage News Daily had the average top-tier 30-year fixed at 6.78% on Monday, August 24, up one basis point on the day, while their early Tuesday MBS tracker showed only minimal positive pressure for rate sheets. At the same time, Treasury trading overnight stayed fragile, with the 10-year yield still elevated even as it bounced around the mid-4.7% area. So the best way to frame the open is not "rates are falling." The better frame is "the market is trying to steady itself, but it has not earned a real victory lap."

Why that matters is simple. When rates stop getting worse, that helps psychology. But when they stop getting worse at a high level, affordability is still the real issue. That means borrowers are still payment-sensitive, pre-approval math still matters, and lenders are still unlikely to hand out aggressive pricing just because the bond market feels slightly less panicked for a few hours.

What this means in plain English is that today is a stability story, not a relief story. The market is telling us that the worst of the immediate spike may be cooling, but financing costs are still expensive enough to knock marginal buyers out and make refinancers demand a very clear reason to act. In other words, this is not a "wait a week and everything gets better" setup. This is a "make clean decisions with the market you actually have" setup.

What to say to customers today is this: "The market feels a little less jumpy this morning, but mortgage rates are still elevated. So if the home works and the payment works, certainty still has value. If the payment does not work yet, we should solve for structure instead of hoping headlines fix it for us."

My lock-or-float guidance today is practical. If the borrower is inside 15 days, has a thin DTI, needs seller concessions to hold together, or is emotionally done with volatility, locking still makes more sense than gambling on a perfect rally. If the borrower is more than 30 days out and has room in the payment, a cautious float can be defended, but only if you explain that 10:00 a.m. data today and inflation data tomorrow can move rate sheets quickly. This is still a defensive float environment, not a casual one.

The key events and times today are real. At 10:00 a.m. Eastern on Tuesday, August 25, the Conference Board releases August consumer confidence and the Census Bureau releases July new home sales. Also at 10:00 a.m. Eastern, the latest home price data hits through Case-Shiller and FHFA. Then at 1:00 p.m. Eastern, the Treasury auctions the 2-year note. On Wednesday, August 26, revised second-quarter GDP, durable goods, personal income, and PCE inflation are due at 8:30 a.m. Eastern. On Friday, August 28, final August consumer sentiment and Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks are the bigger headline risk.

Here is the market and housing context behind the script. Freddie Mac's latest weekly survey showed the 30-year fixed averaging 6.65% as of Thursday, August 20. Mortgage News Daily's daily reading was higher at 6.78% on Monday, August 24, which tells you consumer-facing pricing still feels tighter than the weekly averages suggest. On the housing side, the latest available official data before this morning's 10:00 a.m. releases still points to a market under pressure: July housing starts fell 12.4%, single-family starts fell 9.9%, and July existing-home sales slipped 1.7% to roughly a 4.05 million annual pace. Builder sentiment improved a touch in August, but builders are still leaning on incentives and price cuts, which tells you demand is not broken because buyers forgot how to buy. Demand is constrained because affordability is still doing damage.

The competitive context for online quote objections matters even more in this kind of market. When a borrower says, "I saw a lower rate online," do not fight the screenshot. Translate it. Say: "You probably did see a lower headline rate. The question is whether it assumes points, a shorter lock, perfect credit, lower escrows, or a scenario that changes the second your real file hits underwriting." In this market, the cleanest win is not proving the internet is wrong. It is proving that your quote survives contact with reality.

The pipeline play today has to include the refi angle. This is not a broad refi-wave market, but it is still a strong targeted-refi market. Go look for borrowers with high-rate second liens, clients carrying revolving debt that could be cleaned up with disciplined cash-out math, FHA borrowers who may be closer to dropping into better conventional execution, and homeowners whose prior temporary buydowns are setting up a payment reset later this year. The opportunity is not "rates are low again." The opportunity is "this borrower has a specific pain point we can solve right now."

One rookie mistake to avoid today is using market improvement language that is technically true but practically misleading. Saying, "Bonds are a little better this morning," without immediately adding, "but mortgage pricing is still high," sets false expectations. Newer LOs lose trust when they narrate the market like a trader instead of translating it like an advisor.

One advanced angle for experienced originators is this: separate direction from spread. Even when Treasury yields calm down, mortgage rates do not automatically improve one-for-one because lenders are still pricing mortgage-specific risk, servicing value, and pipeline protection. That is sharper language for veteran LOs, and it keeps you from overpromising every time the 10-year flashes green.

Borrower talk tracks for today should sound like this. "I would rather help you make a strong decision in a tough market than chase a perfect market that may never show up." "If the payment works today, we can always improve the debt later." "If the payment does not work today, that is not failure. That just means we need a better structure, a different price point, or a different timeline." That language lowers pressure without sounding passive.

One concrete borrower example: if you are talking to a first-time buyer at a $400,000 to $450,000 price point who is rattled by seeing rates near the high end of the year's range, do not start with rate defense. Start with decision control. Say: "Let me show you the monthly payment with today's real numbers, then let me show you what changes if we use seller help, a smaller buydown, or a different purchase price. Once we know which payment band actually works, the rate conversation gets much easier." That turns fear into a framework.

One thing to watch today is whether the 10:00 a.m. data confirms a consumer who is tiring out and a housing market that is still grinding lower, or whether one of those reports surprises stronger and pushes yields back up. If stronger data hits while inflation worries are already in the background, lenders will have an easy excuse to stay defensive.

The coaching close for the team is short. Stop trying to sound bullish. Sound useful. In a market like this, the loan officer who wins is the one who can explain why the market feels tight, what the borrower can still control, and what the next smart move is before the borrower starts shopping for certainty somewhere else.
